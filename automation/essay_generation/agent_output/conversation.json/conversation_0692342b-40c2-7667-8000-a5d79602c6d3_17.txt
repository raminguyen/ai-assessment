- `input` + `click` → Fill form field and submit/search in one step
- `input` + `input` → Fill multiple form fields
- `click` + `click` → Navigate through multi-step flows (when the page does not navigate between clicks)
- `scroll` with pages 10 + `extract` → Scroll to the bottom of the page to load more content before extracting structured data
- File operations + browser actions
Do not try multiple different paths in one step. Always have one clear goal per step.
Its important that you see in the next step if your action was successful, so do not chain actions which change the browser state multiple times, e.g.
- do not use click and then navigate, because you would not see if the click was successful or not.

- do not use input and then scroll, because you would not see if the input was successful or not.
</efficiency_guidelines>

			Crucially, the Professor is also being operantly conditioned. This aspect of the human-animal relationship is often overlooked but is vital for understanding the persistence of the interaction. When the cats meow loudly and weave between his legs, they are creating a stimulus that can be perceivably aversive (noise, tripping hazard) or demanding. When the Professor opens the can and places it on the floor, the meowing usually stops, and the erratic movement ceases as the cats eat. For the Professor, the cessation of the loud demands acts as
			[10340]<em />
				negative reinforcement
			. He performs a specific behavior (feeding) to remove an unpleasant or high-pressure stimulus (the demanding behavior of the cats).
		[10367]<p />
			Simultaneously, the purring and rubbing can act as
			[10341]<em />
				positive reinforcement
			for the Professor. The tactile sensation of a cat rubbing against one’s legs and the auditory cue of purring are often perceived as affection. If the Professor enjoys this affection, he is positively reinforced to open the can, as doing so elicits these "loving" behaviors. As noted in reviews of human-animal interactions, this mutual reinforcement creates a dyadic relationship where both parties are effectively training each other (Hosey & Melfi, 2014). The human provides food to gain affection or peace; the animal provides affection or peace to gain food.
		[10368]<h2 level=2 />
			Social Learning: Observation and Facilitation
		[10370]<p />
			The third mechanism, social learning, adds a layer of complexity, particularly if there are multiple cats. Social learning theory, championed by Albert Bandura, suggests that individuals can learn new behaviors or information by observing others. While cats are often stereotyped as solitary, they are facultatively social and capable of observational learning.
		[10372]<p />
			If Professor Catlove has a multi-cat household, it is highly likely that social facilitation is occurring. Social facilitation refers to an increase in the frequency or intensity of a behavior when performed in the presence of others doing the same thing. One cat hearing the can opener and running might trigger the other cats to run, even if the others were dozing and missed the initial cue. The sight of one cat running serves as a secondary cue for the others.
		[10374]<p />
			Furthermore, younger or newer cats in the household may have learned the specific routine—rubbing against legs to beg—by observing the older, established cats. In a classic study, Adler (1955) demonstrated that cats could learn to solve problems by observing a "demonstrator" cat, although the effectiveness of this learning varied. While cats may not imitate as precisely as primates, they are sensitive to the actions of conspecifics. If a kitten observes an older cat meowing and subsequently receiving food, the kitten may attempt to mimic the vocalization, associating the older cat's success with the vocal behavior.
		[10376]<p />
			More recent research has expanded this to include social referencing between cats and humans. Merola et al. (2015) found that cats look to their owners for emotional cues when navigating uncertain situations, a key component of social learning. In the morning routine, if one cat hesitates but sees another cat confidently engaging with the Professor, the hesitant cat is likely to follow suit, having learned that the environment (the kitchen) and the agent (the Professor) are safe and resource-rich.
		[10378]<h2 level=2 />
			Conclusion
		[10380]<p />
			The morning scene in Professor Catlove’s kitchen is far more than a simple biological event; it is a symphony of learned behaviors. Classical conditioning sets the stage, triggering the cats’ physiological anticipation at the sound of the can opener. Operant conditioning drives the action, with the cats employing vocalizations and physical contact to secure food, while simultaneously training the Professor to feed them promptly to secure peace or affection. Finally, social learning amplifies these behaviors, as the cats take cues from one another to coordinate their mass descent upon the kitchen.
		[10382]<p />
			Understanding these three mechanisms highlights the cognitive complexity of domestic animals and the reciprocal nature of the human-pet bond. We often think of ourselves as the masters of our pets, but through the subtle mechanisms of conditioning, we are just as often their subjects, trained by purrs and meows to serve breakfast on time.
